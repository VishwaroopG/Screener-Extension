Two governments can have the same debt-to-GDP ratio and very different financing risks. One may have long-dated domestic-currency borrowing and a broad investor base. The other may need to refinance substantial foreign-currency debt soon. The debt headline is the beginning of the analysis.

Separate the deficit from the debt stock

A deficit is a flow over a period; debt is an accumulated stock. The primary budget balance excludes interest payments, helping distinguish current revenue and spending decisions from the financing cost of earlier borrowing. The IMF identifies growth, interest rates, primary balances and existing debt as central ingredients in debt sustainability.

A government can reduce its primary deficit and still face a rising total interest bill. Equally, a debt ratio can fall even while nominal debt rises if nominal GDP grows faster. Looking only at the currency value of outstanding debt misses that denominator.

The interest-growth relationship

A simplified debt-dynamics calculation compares the effective interest rate on existing debt with nominal GDP growth. When the interest rate is higher, the government generally needs a stronger primary balance to stabilise its debt ratio, other things equal.

For illustration, begin with debt equal to 80% of GDP. With a 6% effective nominal interest rate and 5% nominal GDP growth, the interest-growth contribution adds about 0.76 percentage points to the debt ratio before the primary balance and other adjustments: 80 multiplied by 0.01, divided by 1.05. A primary deficit would add further pressure.

This is an accounting illustration, not a forecast or a safe-debt threshold. Exchange-rate movements, asset transactions, cash balances and other stock-flow adjustments can change the result.

Why refinancing creates a delay

The yield on a newly issued bond is different from the average cost of the entire debt stock. With fixed-rate borrowing, higher market yields usually feed into interest expense as debt matures and new borrowing occurs. A long maturity profile can spread that adjustment over time.

Floating-rate debt reprices more quickly. Foreign-currency debt can become harder to service when the local currency depreciates. These are reasons to read a debt-management report alongside the budget rather than treating one auction as a complete picture.

How fiscal pressure can reach listed businesses

The following are possible channels rather than inevitable outcomes. Higher sovereign yields may raise corporate financing costs. Spending restraint may affect contractors' orders, while delayed government payments can affect their working capital. Tax changes may alter household spending or company cash flows.

Productive public investment may also improve transport, power or logistics over time. The composition and execution of spending matter as much as its size. A large project announcement does not establish that the economic return will exceed its financing cost.

A compact fiscal reading list

  • The primary balance and whether estimates rely on temporary revenues.
  • Interest payments relative to revenue, alongside debt relative to GDP.
  • The maturity profile, currency mix and upcoming refinancing needs.
  • The growth assumptions and sensitivity analysis behind the projections.

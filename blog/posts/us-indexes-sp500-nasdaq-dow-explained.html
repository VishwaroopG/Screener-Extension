Bottom line: the S&P 500 tracks 500 large US companies and is the default scoreboard for the American market. The Nasdaq (usually the Nasdaq-100) leans heavily toward big technology stocks, so it swings harder. The Dow follows just 30 blue-chip stocks and moves the least. If you only watch one, watch the S&P 500 — and Ticker Screener keeps all three scrolling in your browser tab.

What is the S&P 500?

The S&P 500 groups 500 of the largest publicly traded US companies — Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon and hundreds of names you never hear about. Because it is weighted by company size, the biggest stocks pull it the most. Most American 401(k)s and index funds mirror it, which is why headlines treat it as the market. When someone asks "how did the market do today", this is the number they mean.

Best for: anyone who wants one honest snapshot of US equities instead of cherry-picked headlines.

How is the Nasdaq different?

The Nasdaq-100 holds the 100 largest non-financial stocks listed on the Nasdaq exchange — a lineup dominated by technology: chipmakers, software, cloud and consumer tech. That concentration is a double-edged sword: in rallies it outruns everything, in sell-offs it falls the fastest. If your portfolio is tech-heavy, the Nasdaq is your personal benchmark.

Best for: growth investors and anyone holding Mag-7 or semiconductor stocks.

What about the Dow Jones?

The Dow tracks only 30 established blue-chip companies and is weighted by share price rather than company size — an old-fashioned formula that makes it the calmest of the three. It rarely tells you anything the S&P 500 doesn't, but it is still quoted everywhere, so knowing it saves confusion when the two disagree on a given day.

Best for: traditionalists and anyone who wants the least dramatic daily number.

Frequently asked questions

Which index should beginners follow?

The S&P 500. It is the broadest of the three and the one your index funds most likely track.

Why do the three indices disagree?

Different members and different weighting. A tech sell-off sinks the Nasdaq while the Dow barely moves; a rally in one mega-cap stock lifts the S&P 500 more than the Dow.

TL;DR: Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have signed a landmark joint declaration demanding the right to hold independence referendums. The First Ministers of all three devolved nations — SNP leader John Swinney, Plaid Cymru's Rhun ap Iorwerth, and Sinn Féin's Michelle O'Neill — met in Cardiff on September 15, 2026, to sign a memorandum of understanding. The move puts unprecedented pressure on PM Andy Burnham, who has rejected calls for a second Scottish independence vote. What happened in Cardiff?

On September 15, 2026, the First Ministers of Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland met in Cardiff and signed a joint declaration — the first time since devolution that pro-independence factions are simultaneously in the majority across all three nations. The memorandum of understanding demands that Westminster prepare, plan and facilitate constitutional change, and that each nation's voters have the final say on their future.

"Our nations have the right to self-determination. No Westminster government has the right to block democracy or undermine the principle that our people will decide their own future," the leaders said in the memorandum.

Who signed the agreement?

  • John Swinney — SNP leader and First Minister of Scotland, pushing for a second independence referendum after the 2014 vote saw Scotland narrowly remain in the UK
  • Rhun ap Iorwerth — Plaid Cymru leader and First Minister of Wales, advocating Welsh independence and EU membership
  • Michelle O'Neill — Sinn Féin vice president and First Minister of Northern Ireland, supporting a border poll on a united Ireland

The agreement covers four areas: economy, energy, Europe and international cooperation, and self-determination. All three parties agree their countries should be in the European Union — Scotland and Wales as new members, and Northern Ireland as part of the Republic of Ireland.

What did Trump say about it?

US President Donald Trump waded into the debate during a visit to the Irish Open golf tournament. He reiterated his support for Northern Ireland breaking from the UK to unify with the Republic of Ireland: "You have Northern Ireland and you have Ireland and it would seem to me that one of the naturals of all time is to put them together." When asked about Scottish independence, Trump said: "That one I won't talk about yet."

How has the UK government responded?

PM Andy Burnham rejected calls for a legal framework for a Scottish independence referendum, saying: "Independence and a referendum are off limits as it would take our focus away from growing the economy and helping families with the cost of living." A Downing Street spokesman said the PM believes "strongly in the Union."

Burnham has scheduled a summit with Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland for October, but the joint declaration significantly raises the stakes.

What do the polls show?

Support for independence varies by nation:

  • Scotland: 47% support independence — close to the 50% threshold but not yet a majority
  • Northern Ireland: 36% support independence — though support for a united Ireland is higher when framed as reunification with the Republic
  • Wales: 32% support independence — the lowest of the three, but growing

The Scottish Parliament has already endorsed the SNP's call for a second independence referendum, with SNP and Green MSPs voting in favour.

Why do investors care?

UK political uncertainty is weighing on markets. The FTSE 100 has underperformed US and European peers for years, and the prospect of a fragmented UK adds another layer of risk. Investors need to watch:

  • Currency risk — Scottish independence would likely mean a new currency, creating uncertainty for UK assets
  • Trade friction — an independent Scotland or Wales outside the UK would face new border arrangements
  • EU membership — all three nations want to join or rejoin the EU, which could reshape trade relationships
  • Defence spending — a break-up of the UK would raise questions about NATO membership and military bases

Frequently asked questions

Will Scotland become independent?

Support for independence is at 47%, close to but not yet a majority. The SNP wants a second referendum, but the UK government has refused to grant one. The joint declaration with Wales and Northern Ireland raises pressure, but a referendum is not imminent.

Will Wales become independent?

Support for Welsh independence is at 32%, the lowest of the three nations. However, First Minister Rhun ap Iorwerth is a strong advocate, and the movement is gaining momentum.

What about Northern Ireland?

First Minister Michelle O'Neill supports a border poll on a united Ireland. Trump has backed Irish unity, saying it would be "one of the naturals of all time" to unite Northern Ireland with the Republic. However, Unionist factions oppose the move.

How does this affect UK markets?

Political uncertainty weighs on investor confidence. The FTSE 100 trades at a discount to US and European peers, and the prospect of a fragmented UK adds currency, trade and geopolitical risk. Investors should monitor developments closely.

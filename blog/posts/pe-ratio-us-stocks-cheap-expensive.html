Bottom line: the P/E ratio (price-to-earnings) tells you how many dollars investors pay for each dollar of a company's yearly profit. A P/E of 20 means $20 per $1 of earnings. Low usually means cheap, high usually means expensive — but the word usually does a lot of work. This guide shows how to read it, where it lies, and how Ticker Screener turns it into a one-glance verdict.

How do you calculate P/E?

Divide the share price by earnings per share over the last twelve months. Nvidia at roughly $218 with earnings near $8 per share trades around 27 times earnings. The S&P 500 as a whole has historically sat between 15 and 25 — below that range the market looks cheap, far above it looks stretched.

Best for: beginners who want one number that separates a bargain from hype.

When does P/E lie to you?

  • Loss-making companies have no meaningful P/E at all — a biotech burning cash can look "cheap" or show nothing.
  • Different sectors, different norms. A utility at a P/E of 12 and a software stock at 35 can both be fairly priced; compare peers, never the whole market.
  • One-off earnings spikes (a big asset sale) can make a stock look cheap for exactly one quarter.

Best for: anyone who has ever bought a "cheap" stock and watched it get cheaper.

What is a good P/E for US stocks?

There is no magic number, but rough US landmarks help: under ~15 is value territory, 15–25 is the broad-market norm, and above ~30 means investors are paying for serious future growth. Always stack the stock against its sector peers — that single comparison catches most mistakes.

Frequently asked questions

Is a low P/E always a buy?

No. It can signal a bargain — or a business in decline. Check earnings trend, debt and peers before deciding.

Why do some great companies have sky-high P/E ratios?

Investors pay for expected future earnings, not past ones. A fast grower at a P/E of 50 is a bet that earnings will triple.

Is this financial advice?

No. P/E is a starting lens, not a verdict on its own. Always do your own research.

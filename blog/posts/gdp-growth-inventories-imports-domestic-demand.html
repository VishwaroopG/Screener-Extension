A strong GDP headline can hide weak customer demand. A soft headline can coexist with households and businesses still spending. The difference often lies in inventories, imports and the mix of expenditure behind the aggregate. Reading that mix is more useful than treating one growth number as a verdict on every listed company.

Start with what GDP measures

Gross domestic product measures production within an economy. In the expenditure approach, it brings together consumption, investment, government spending and net exports. The US Bureau of Economic Analysis also publishes real final sales to private domestic purchasers: consumer spending plus private fixed investment. That narrower measure helps separate private final demand from inventory changes, government spending and trade.

Neither series is automatically the correct one for every business. A government contractor cares about public purchases, and an exporter cares about foreign demand. The narrower series answers a particular question rather than replacing GDP.

Inventories count before the customer buys

Imagine a hypothetical manufacturer producing 100 machines but selling 90. The ten unsold machines enter inventory investment. Their production contributes to GDP even though the cash from a final buyer has not yet arrived.

Next quarter, the manufacturer produces 90 and sells 100 by drawing down that stock. Sales improve, yet inventory investment can subtract from measured growth. This is why a retailer's revenue and the inventory contribution to GDP can move in different directions.

There is another subtlety: growth contributions depend on changes in inventory investment. Inventories can keep rising but contribute less to growth if the pace of accumulation slows. A large warehouse balance and a positive contribution to GDP growth are different concepts.

Imports need careful interpretation

Imports are subtracted because foreign production can already be included in recorded domestic expenditure. The adjustment removes that foreign content; it does not mean buying an imported machine mechanically destroys an equivalent amount of domestic output. BEA explains the accounting in its guide to the expenditure approach.

For example, imported equipment can accompany a domestic factory expansion. That import may be a clue about future productive capacity. Alternatively, a fall in imports can reflect weaker household spending. The net-export contribution alone cannot distinguish these stories.

Match the macro release to company evidence

  • Retailers: Compare sales volumes, inventory turnover and markdowns.
  • Manufacturers: Read new orders alongside finished-goods stocks and capacity use.
  • Capital-goods suppliers: Separate announced projects from orders, deliveries and customer payments.
  • Exporters: Examine demand in destination markets and changes in product mix.

These are research questions derived from the accounting, not automatic sector trades. A company may have a very different geographic or customer mix from the national average.

Do not mix growth conventions

US quarterly releases commonly emphasise annualised quarter-on-quarter real growth. Indian GDP reporting often highlights year-on-year growth. Compare the same convention and period before ranking economies. Also distinguish real output from nominal revenue: higher prices can lift a company's sales without a matching increase in volume.

A good release-day note records the headline, its components and the earlier estimate. Later revisions can matter as much as the initial surprise.

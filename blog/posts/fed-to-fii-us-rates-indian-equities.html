Bottom line: in September 2026 the Fed hiked to 3.75–4.00%, the US 10-year pushed above 5.10%, and foreign investors pulled ₹20,974 crore from Indian equities in just 18 days. If you cover Indian small-caps, you cannot model earnings without modelling Washington. Here is the transmission chain an equity research analyst actually uses.

The 2026 setup in one paragraph

Year-to-date, foreign portfolio investors (FPIs) have sold roughly ₹2.45 lakh crore (about $25.5 billion) — already 48% more than in all of 2025. September alone saw over ₹18,500 crore of net selling by the 25th, while domestic institutions (DIIs) bought over ₹52,600 crore, cushioning the fall. The Nifty slipped about 3.9% in September, the rupee touched 95.9–96 per dollar, Brent crude held above $100, and the Fed's September 17 hike was unanimous with signals of one more hike this year.

The three channels: yields, currency, risk appetite

Channel A — Relative yield

When risk-free dollars yield 5% or more, the India–US yield gap compresses. A global allocator comparing an Indian small-cap at 18x forward earnings with a US investment-grade bond asks a harder question. Cost of equity in professional models is typically US 10-year + India equity risk premium + small-cap illiquidity premium — so a 100 bps move in the US 10-year can shave 12–18% off a long-duration small-cap DCF.

Channel B — Currency return erosion

FPIs book returns in dollars. A 5–6% annual rupee depreciation wipes out a large part of a 12% rupee return. That is why secondary-market selling coexists with selective primary-market buying (over ₹5,500 crore of FPI IPO participation on September 24 alone): new listings with pricing power can clear the currency hurdle, while broad beta cannot.

Channel C — Crude and the current account

Brent above $100 hits India twice: the import bill widens, and input costs (diesel for haulage, petrochemical feedstock for laminates) squeeze company EBITDA directly.

Analyst rule: before updating any target price in a Fed week, update three cells first — US 10-year, USD/INR, Brent. Only then earnings.

Why small and mid-caps move more than the Nifty

  • Thinner floats: even marginal FPI selling gaps liquidity and doubles bid-ask spreads in risk-off weeks.
  • Longer duration: small-cap value is back-ended — capex today, cash flows in FY28–30. Higher discount rates punish back-ended cash flows most.
  • Slower pass-through: large-caps pass on fuel and rate costs faster; EPC sub-contractors and laminate distributors absorb one to two quarters of margin pain.

A screening overlay for risk-off regimes

  • Leverage: Net Debt / EBITDA below 1.5x with no major bullet maturities in 12 months — refinancing at higher yields kills project SPVs.
  • Cash conversion: operating cash flow above 70% of EBITDA with receivable days flat — working capital stretches when payments slow.
  • Real returns: ROCE above stressed cost of equity + 300 bps, so value creation survives a 5%+ US 10-year.
  • Real orders: funded, appointed-date order book only — unfunded pipeline announcements are not revenue.

What to watch next

  1. Fed signals: one more hike is priced in — any dovish dissent can restart EM flows fast (July–August saw nearly ₹50,000 crore return on pause hopes alone).
  2. The 5% level on the US 10-year: sustained breaks above ~5.10% have historically triggered FPI secondary selling; a fall below 4.5% re-opens small-cap re-rating.
  3. The rupee-96 plus Brent-$100 combo: if both persist, margins guide lower and EBITDA sensitivity tables shift 150–200 bps down.
  4. Primary vs secondary divergence: continued FPI IPO participation alongside secondary selling means a stock-picker's market, not a beta market.

US monetary policy is an input — not background noise — to Indian small-cap valuation. Model it explicitly, stress your discount rates, demand stronger balance sheets, and you can buy the dips with conviction instead of hope.

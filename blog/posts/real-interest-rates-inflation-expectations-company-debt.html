An unchanged interest rate can become more restrictive as inflation falls. A rate cut can offer less relief than it first appears if expected inflation falls faster. The missing variable is the real interest rate: the cost of borrowing measured against changes in purchasing power.

Nominal rates and real rates answer different questions

The nominal rate is the rate agreed on a loan or deposit. A real rate adjusts for inflation. The ECB's explanation of nominal and real interest rates gives the familiar approximation: nominal interest minus inflation.

For decisions about the future, expected inflation is the relevant comparison. A calculation using realised inflation answers a backward-looking question about what actually happened. De Nederlandsche Bank describes real rates using expected inflation, which is useful when thinking about a new borrowing or saving decision.

Two hypothetical rate cuts

Suppose a nominal borrowing rate starts at 6% and expected inflation at 4%. The approximate real rate is 2%. If the nominal rate falls to 5.5% while expected inflation falls to 2.5%, the real rate rises to roughly 3%. The quoted borrowing rate fell, yet the inflation-adjusted rate increased.

The exact one-period calculation is (1 + nominal rate) divided by (1 + inflation), minus one. Subtraction is an approximation that is usually convenient at modest rates. Neither calculation tells you whether a rate is above the economy's uncertain neutral real rate.

A company still pays the nominal cash bill

Real-rate reasoning does not make contractual payments disappear. A business must meet its interest and principal obligations in money. Its own selling prices may also rise more slowly or quickly than the consumer inflation index used in a macro calculation.

Consider a hypothetical supplier with fixed nominal debt payments. If its sales prices stagnate while wages and materials remain costly, debt service may become harder even as national inflation falls. A business with stronger volume growth or more cash can experience the same environment differently.

The policy rate is not the company borrowing rate

A corporate borrowing rate can be thought of as a benchmark yield plus compensation for credit and other risks. If the benchmark falls but the credit spread widens, refinancing can remain expensive. Maturity, currency, collateral and fixed-versus-floating terms all affect the outcome.

This is a framework for reading disclosures, not a prediction of spread changes. Check actual financing terms rather than assuming a central-bank announcement passes through one-for-one to every borrower.

Questions to ask before calling lower rates bullish

  • Have inflation expectations changed over the same horizon as the rate being compared?
  • Does the company have debt repricing soon, or fixed funding for several years?
  • Are credit spreads and bank lending terms improving too?
  • Will lower financing costs be offset by weaker sales or margins?

Market valuations can respond to both discount rates and earnings expectations. A lower nominal yield therefore has no guaranteed effect on a share price. Separate the financing story from the business outlook.

A lower inflation number can be welcome news even while the weekly shopping bill keeps rising. That is because inflation measures the speed of price increases, while households pay the accumulated price level. Understanding the distinction helps explain why consumers, central banks and financial markets sometimes react differently to the same release.

Why the comparison month matters

Annual inflation compares a price index with its level twelve months earlier. An unusually high or low starting point can move the annual rate even when recent monthly increases are fairly ordinary. The European Central Bank explains this mechanism in its historical explainer on inflation and base effects. The arithmetic applies beyond the particular episode discussed there.

Consider a hypothetical index that rises from 100 to 110 over a year. Annual inflation is 10%. During the following year, the index rises to 115.5. Inflation has slowed to 5%, but the basket costs 15.5% more than it did two years earlier. The slower rate does not reverse the earlier increase.

Now imagine an energy price spike dropping out of the twelve-month comparison. Annual inflation may fall sharply without a comparable improvement in the latest month's prices. The opposite can happen when a period of unusually cheap energy leaves the calculation.

Disinflation and deflation describe different outcomes

Disinflation means prices are increasing more slowly. Deflation means the broad price level is falling. Falling prices in one category do not establish economy-wide deflation. The St. Louis Fed's explanation of falling inflation versus falling prices makes this distinction useful for everyday budgets as well as market analysis.

Read three layers of the release

  • The annual rate: How different is the price level from a year ago?
  • Recent momentum: Are the latest seasonally adjusted monthly increases slowing too? Compare several months rather than extrapolating one unusual reading.
  • The composition: Is improvement concentrated in a volatile category, or spreading across goods and services?

Compare like with like. An unadjusted monthly movement around a holiday is different from a seasonally adjusted estimate. Likewise, annualising one month magnifies both its signal and its noise. Revisions and changing seasonal patterns can alter the apparent trend.

How the same inflation surprise can affect businesses differently

The following is an analytical scenario, not a prediction. Suppose freight and packaging costs ease while household wages keep growing. A consumer business might gain from both lower input costs and recovering purchasing power. But if it cuts selling prices faster than costs decline, its margin can still shrink. Revenue growth also slows when earlier price increases stop contributing.

A lender faces a different set of questions: how quickly do loan yields reprice, how sticky are deposit costs, and are borrowers becoming more able to service debt? A favourable inflation headline alone cannot answer them.

A useful question before the next release

Ask what would change your interpretation. A falling annual rate alongside persistent monthly pressure suggests a different outlook from broad monthly cooling supported by stable employment. Relate that distinction to a company's sales volumes, pricing and costs before drawing a conclusion about its shares.

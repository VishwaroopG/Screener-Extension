Bottom line: with the US 10-year above 5.10% and the Fed at 3.75–4.00%, long-duration Nasdaq growth stocks face simple math: future earnings discounted at 5%+ are worth less today. Quality cash-flow compounders survive; story stocks with FY28–30 payoffs re-rate hardest.

Why 5% changes everything

A growth stock is a long-duration asset — most of its value sits in distant cash flows. At a 2% discount rate, $100 in 2030 is worth ~$85 today; at 5.5%, it is worth ~$68. That 20% haircut lands before the company misses a single quarter. It hits the Nasdaq harder than the Dow because tech cash flows stretch furthest into the future, and small-caps hardest of all because illiquidity adds its own premium.

Best for: growth investors wondering why solid companies fall when "nothing changed" in earnings.

The 2026 transmission in one paragraph

The Fed's unanimous September 17 hike to 3.75–4.00% — with one more hike signalled — pushed the 10-year through ~5.10%, compressed the equity risk premium, lifted corporate refinancing costs and strengthened the dollar. Result: P/E compression for non-earning growth, wider credit spreads for levered balance sheets, and rotation toward cash-generative megacaps that self-fund AI capex.

A growth-stock screen for 5%+ regimes

  • Self-funding: free cash flow positive with capex covered internally — no equity raises into weak markets.
  • Pricing power: gross margins flat or up with Brent above $100; software with 80%+ gross margins clears this bar easiest.
  • Leverage: net debt / EBITDA below 1.5x, no wall of maturities in 12 months.
  • Real ROCE: ROCE above stressed cost of equity + 300 bps, so value creation survives the discount-rate shock.

Frequently asked questions

Should I sell all growth stocks above 5%?

No. Shorten duration, demand cash flow, cut leverage — do not abandon compounding businesses at better prices.

What level re-opens re-rating?

Historically, a sustained fall in the 10-year back toward ~4.5% re-opens small and mid-cap growth multiples. Watch it, do not predict it.

Is this financial advice?

No. Rate-sensitivity explainer only. Always do your own research.

A central bank can hold its policy rate steady while businesses find credit harder to obtain. Banks may require stronger collateral, lower leverage or more expensive terms. This is the credit channel: the availability of finance can change even when the headline interest rate does not.

Credit has a price and an approval decision

A borrowing decision involves more than the advertised rate. A company needs a lender willing to advance the money, a sufficient loan amount, workable repayment terms and manageable covenants. A reduction in any of those can constrain investment or working capital.

The Federal Reserve's Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey on Bank Lending Practices asks banks about changes in lending standards, terms and loan demand. Keeping demand separate from supply is essential: fewer loans can reflect fewer applicants, stricter lenders, or both.

Read a tightening percentage correctly

In survey measures reported as a net percentage tightening, the number reflects the balance of respondents reporting tightening against those reporting easing. It is not the percentage of borrowers rejected or the percentage decline in lending. Check the individual release and question wording before interpreting a chart.

A falling net-tightening reading can also mean banks are becoming restrictive more slowly. It does not necessarily mean the accumulated restrictions have been reversed. The distinction resembles a car slowing down while still travelling forward.

How a credit squeeze reaches operations

Consider a hypothetical distributor that pays suppliers in 30 days but collects from customers in 75. It finances the gap with a revolving credit line. If its lender cuts that line, the company may have to reduce stock, negotiate supplier terms or accept fewer orders even if underlying demand is unchanged.

An exporter with letters of credit and currency hedges faces another set of constraints. A developer may depend on refinancing a large maturity. These examples are possible transmission mechanisms, not evidence that any named borrower is under stress.

There can also be a delay. A company may initially use cash or draw an existing facility, postponing the visible effect of tighter standards. Reported loan growth therefore need not turn at the same moment as a lending survey.

What to inspect at company level

  • Maturity schedule: How much debt needs refinancing, and when?
  • Liquidity: Is cash available where obligations fall due, and are facilities committed?
  • Interest coverage: Can operating earnings absorb higher financing costs?
  • Working capital: Are inventories or unpaid customer invoices tying up more cash?

A cash-rich business and a leveraged competitor may experience the same credit cycle very differently. Banks themselves also face trade-offs: tighter standards can protect future loan quality but constrain lending growth. Deposit funding costs and existing credit losses still matter.

Use the survey as an early question

The Fed's survey archive allows comparisons across lending categories and past periods. Treat those comparisons as a way to frame research. A survey of US banks is not a direct measurement of Indian bank credit, and a historical relationship is not a countdown to recession.

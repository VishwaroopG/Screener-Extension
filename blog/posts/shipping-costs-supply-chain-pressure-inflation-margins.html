A shipping disruption can reach a company's accounts long before consumers see an empty shelf. Longer voyages tie up inventory, freight charges change landed costs, and delayed components interrupt production. These channels make logistics an important link between world events and corporate cash flow.

Separate freight prices from demand

A rising freight rate can reflect strong orders, reduced shipping capacity or disrupted routes. The causes matter. Strong demand may support manufacturers' sales, while a disruption can raise costs without improving final demand.

The New York Fed's Global Supply Chain Pressure Index combines transport-cost information with manufacturing indicators. It gives broader context than a single route quote. Its level is expressed relative to historical conditions, so it should not be read as a percentage increase in shipping costs.

UNCTAD's Review of Maritime Transport 2025 describes how historical route diversions increased voyage distances and operating costs. The mechanism is useful; it does not establish that the same disruption or freight rate persists today.

The working-capital channel

Imagine a hypothetical importer selling $1 million of goods a month. If an extra half-month of goods must remain in transit, roughly $500,000 more sales-equivalent inventory is tied up. The actual cash requirement depends on purchase cost, supplier credit and customer deposits, but the example shows why delivery time matters separately from freight expense.

A company may also hold extra safety stock to protect delivery commitments. That can make operations more resilient while weakening near-term cash conversion. If demand later disappoints, the extra inventory can become a markdown or obsolescence problem.

Retail inflation may respond with a lag

Some companies book freight under long-term contracts; others use spot rates. Existing inventory, hedges and supplier agreements can delay the impact on earnings. Firms with pricing power may pass costs through, while others absorb them or change sourcing.

These are analytical possibilities, not a universal timetable. IMF research on shipping costs and inflation finds a relationship across countries, but an estimated average effect is not a precise forecast for a particular company or shipment.

Different businesses face different exposures

  • Importers: Landed costs, transit times and supplier payment terms.
  • Exporters: Delivery reliability and whether the buyer or seller pays freight.
  • Manufacturers: Dependence on components that can stop an entire production line.
  • Logistics providers: Contract pricing, fuel costs and available capacity.

Even within shipping, container, dry-bulk and tanker markets are distinct. A move in one index does not establish the cost of moving every kind of cargo. Match the indicator to the business's actual products and routes.

What to read in the next earnings release

Look for inventory days, expedited freight costs, delivery backlogs and cash from operations. Compare management's explanation with margins and working capital. Improving transport conditions may release cash even before they produce a visible increase in profit.
